= Cryptography

Cryptography is the discipline of securing information and communication against
adversaries. The name comes from the Greek _kryptós_ ("hidden") and _gráphein_
("to write"), and it is one of the oldest applied sciences in
xref:computer-science.adoc[*computer science*]. Its concern is not only keeping
data secret, but also ensuring that data has not been altered, that parties are
who they claim to be, and that actions cannot later be denied.

Cryptography is a foundational technique within
xref:security.adoc[*security*], but it is narrower than security as a whole.
Security also covers operational and procedural controls — access policy,
patching, and physical access — that cryptography cannot address. Conversely,
cryptography supplies the mathematical primitives on which much of security is
built.

== Core goals

Cryptographic systems are designed to provide one or more of the following
properties.

* *Confidentiality.* Only authorized parties can read the data. This is the
  familiar goal of xref:encryption.adoc[*encryption*], which transforms
  plaintext into ciphertext that is meaningless without a key.
* *Integrity.* The data has not been modified in transit or at rest. Detecting
  deliberate modification, as opposed to accidental corruption, requires
  xref:hashing.adoc[*cryptographic hash functions*] rather than ordinary
  xref:checksum.adoc[*checksums*].
* *Authenticity.* The origin of the data can be verified.
  xref:authentication.adoc[*Authentication*] of users and systems leans heavily
  on cryptographic mechanisms such as digital signatures and certificates.
* *Non-repudiation.* The sender cannot later deny having sent the message.
  Digital signatures provide this guarantee, because a signature can only have
  been produced by the holder of the private key.

== Primitives

Cryptography is built from a small set of primitives, each addressing a
different goal. Systems compose these primitives rather than inventing new
ones.

* *Symmetric encryption.* A single secret key both encrypts and decrypts. It is
  fast and suited to bulk data, but the parties must already share the key.
* *Public-key (asymmetric) encryption.* Each party holds a key pair: a private
  key kept secret and a public key shared openly. What one key encrypts, the
  other decrypts. This removes the need to share a secret in advance and
  underpins digital signatures and certificate-based
  xref:authentication.adoc[*authentication*].
* *Cryptographic hash functions.* One-way functions that produce a fixed-size
  digest of any input. They are the basis of tamper detection,
  xref:checksum.adoc[*checksums*], and password storage with
  xref:salt.adoc[*salt*].
* *Message authentication codes (MACs).* A hash keyed with a secret that proves
  both integrity and authenticity in a single value.
* *Digital signatures.* The public-key analogue of a MAC. The signer hashes the
  message and encrypts the digest with their private key; anyone with the
  public key can verify it.
* *Key exchange.* Protocols that let two parties establish a shared secret over
  an insecure channel, eg. Diffie-Hellman.

Most real systems combine several primitives.
xref:envelope-encryption.adoc[*Envelope encryption*], for example, uses a
symmetric data key for the payload and an asymmetric key-encryption key to
protect the data key. Protecting against xref:replay-attack.adoc[*replay
attacks*] typically adds a xref:nonce.adoc[*nonce*] to each message so that
intercepted traffic cannot be retransmitted.

== Cryptography versus encryption

The two terms are often used interchangeably, but they name different things.
Cryptography is the discipline. xref:encryption.adoc[*Encryption*] is one
technique within it — the transformation of plaintext to ciphertext for
confidentiality. Hashing, signatures, and key exchange are also cryptography,
but they are not encryption.
