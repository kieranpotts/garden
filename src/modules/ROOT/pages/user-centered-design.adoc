= User-centered design

User-centered design (UCD) is a design framework in which the goals,
characteristics, environment, tasks, and workflows of the people who will use a
product are given sustained attention at every stage of the design process.
Rather than shaping a product around what is convenient to build, UCD shapes it
around how users actually think and act, so that users are not forced to change
their behavior and expectations to accommodate the product.

The term was coined by Rob Kling in 1977 and was brought into the mainstream of
human–computer interaction by Donald Norman's research group at the University
of California, San Diego. Norman's 1986 collection _User-Centered System
Design_ and his 1988 book _The Design of Everyday Things_ turned the idea into a
widely cited set of principles for good design. Norman argued that well-designed
products simplify the structure of tasks, make the system's conceptual model
visible, map intended results to required actions, and exploit the constraints
a system already offers.

UCD is iterative. The canonical cycle has four phases that repeat rather than run
once.

* *Specify the context of use.* Identify the people who will use the product,
  why they will use it, and the environment they will use it in.
* *Specify requirements.* Translate those needs into
  *xref:requirements-engineering.adoc[requirements]* the product must satisfy.
* *Create design solutions.* Produce
  *xref:prototyping.adoc[prototypes]* and refine them against the requirements.
* *Evaluate.* Test with real or representative users through
  *xref:usability-testing.adoc[usability testing]*, feed the findings back into
  the design, and repeat.

Several artifacts help a team keep the design tied to real users rather than to
the team's own assumptions. A *xref:persona.adoc[persona]* distills user research
into a fictional but evidence-based archetype that the team can design for. A
scenario describes a persona pursuing a goal, grounding design decisions in a
concrete narrative. An *xref:use-case.adoc[essential use case]* abstracts a task
into its intent and the system's response, stripped of interface detail, so the
designer can reason about what the user is trying to do before deciding how the
interface should do it.

The framework is broader than any single technique. It draws on a family of
related traditions, including the Scandinavian cooperative design movement,
participatory design, and contextual design, all of which share the premise that
the people who will live with a system should shape it. UCD is codified in
ISO 9241-210, the international standard for human-centered design processes for
interactive systems.

UCD is sometimes contrasted with approaches that center the system rather than
the person. *xref:teslers-law.adoc[Tesler's law]* captures the underlying
trade-off. Every application has an irreducible amount of complexity, and UCD
holds that the developer should absorb that complexity rather than push it onto
the user. Designing for the whole user experience also means designing for users
who are not the team's default, which is why
*xref:accessibility-testing.adoc[accessibility]* and
*xref:ab-testing.adoc[A/B testing]* sit comfortably inside a UCD process as
evaluation techniques for different audiences and different kinds of question.
