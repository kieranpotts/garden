= Statement of work (SOW)

:link-far-37-6:  https://www.acquisition.gov/far/subpart-37.6

A *statement of work (SOW)* is a document that defines a specific piece of work that a supplier agrees to carry out for a client. It sets out what will be done, what will be delivered, by when, at what price, and how the client will decide that the work is complete.

It's the standard way to scope an engagement with a xref:software-house.adoc[*software house*], a consultancy, or a contractor. It is widely used across both public and private sector procurement.

An SOW usually sits under a larger *services agreement*, which holds the general legal terms of the relationship, such as liability, intellectual property, confidentiality, and termination. Each new project or phase then gets its own SOW, which holds only the commercial and delivery details of that piece of work. This keeps the legal terms from being negotiated again every time, while each engagement is still scoped precisely.

A SOW turns a shared understanding into a written contractual document. A vague SOW is a leading cause of xref:scope-creep.adoc[*scope creep*], payment disputes, and failed engagements. A precise one also forces the hard conversations about scope, cost, and schedule to happen before work starts, which itself can be a cause of delivery failures due to the xref:project-paradox.adoc[*project paradox*]. The challenge is balancing precision against flexibility. A SOW needs enough detail to protect both parties, but not so much that it locks in commitments too early.

A SOW is distinct from a xref:product-requirements-document.adoc[*product requirements document (PRD)*], though both documents may form part of the commercial agreement.

== Typical contents

The structure varies by organization, but most SOWs cover the following topics.

* *Purpose and background.* Why the work is being commissioned, and the business outcome it supports.

* *Scope of work.* The activities the supplier will carry out, and where the work is done.

* *Out-of-scope.* What the supplier will explicitly _not_ do. This is often the most important part of the document for preventing disputes.

* *Deliverables.* The concrete outputs the client will receive, such as software, documentation, designs, or reports.

* *Schedule and milestones.* The timeline, broken into milestones that payments and reviews are often tied to.

* *Acceptance.* The xref:acceptance-criteria.adoc[*acceptance criteria*] each deliverable must meet, and the procedure and time allowed for the client to accept or reject it.

* *Pricing and payment terms.* The commercial model, the rates or fixed fee, and when invoices fall due.

* *Roles, responsibilities, and dependencies.* What each party must provide, such as access, environments, or decisions, for the work to proceed.

* *Assumptions.* The conditions the plan and price depend on, so that a broken assumption gives grounds to renegotiate the SOW.

* *Change control.* How changes to scope are requested, estimated, and approved, usually through a formal *change request* that amends the SOW.

The structure and contents of SOWs varies depending on the nature of the work. SOWs for *time-and-materials* delivery will look different to those based on *fixed-price* contracts, for example.

Where the work includes running a service as well as building one (*build-and-run* model), the ongoing service levels are usually set out in a separate xref:service-level-agreement.adoc[*service level agreement (SLA)*] rather than in the SOW itself.
