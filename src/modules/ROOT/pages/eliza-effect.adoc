= ELIZA effect

:link-weizenbaum-eliza: https://doi.org/10.1145/365153.365168

The ELIZA effect is the human tendency to read understanding, intent, and even feeling into a computer system's output, however shallow the mechanism that produced it. The name comes from ELIZA, a program written by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT in 1966.

ELIZA's best-known script, DOCTOR, imitated a Rogerian psychotherapist by matching keywords in the user's input and reflecting them back as questions: "I am unhappy" became "Why do you think you are unhappy?" There was no model of the user's situation behind this, only a handful of pattern-matching rules. Even so, people confided in it, and some insisted it understood them, even after being told how it worked.

Weizenbaum was disturbed by this, and spent much of his later career arguing against letting machines stand in for human judgment and care.

The effect matters far more now than it did in 1966. A modern xref:large-language-model.adoc[*large language model*] is vastly more fluent than ELIZA, and so is far more convincing. It appears to understand, to reason, to know its own mind, and to feel.

But what produces that surface is statistical inference over tokens learned from human writing — not comprehension, intent, or self-awareness. A model's apparent emotions are generated from the context, and mostly mirror the register the user brought to the conversation. Confident, fluent output invites trust in a model's accuracy, even though models have no internal capability to verify the accuracy of their own output. Furthermore, a model's explanation of its own reasoning reads like introspection, but it is generated after the fact, like any other output, and is not a record of how the answer was reached.

None of this means the technology is not useful, but it means large-language models should be treated as fluent simulators of human output, but output that must be verified before being believed.
