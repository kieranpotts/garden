= Bike shedding (or the law of triviality)

Bike shedding, also known as the *bike-shed effect* or the *law of triviality*,
is the tendency for a group to spend disproportionate time and energy on
trivial matters while neglecting complex, consequential ones. It is a pathology
of collective decision-making: the easier a question is to grasp, the more
people feel qualified to weigh in on it, and the longer it dominates the
discussion.

The term comes from a story in C. Northcote Parkinson's 1958 book _Parkinson's
Law: Or the Pursuit of Progress_. In it, a fictional committee is tasked with
approving three items: a nuclear power plant, an atomic reactor, and a bicycle
shed. The nuclear plant, a project of enormous cost and consequence, is
approved almost without comment, because no member feels competent to question
it. The bicycle shed, by contrast, consumes most of the meeting, as every
member has an opinion on its materials and its color. The committee then spends
still longer arguing over the coffee budget.

The mechanism is straightforward. A complex proposal leaves most members silent,
because they lack the expertise to challenge it and fear exposing that
ignorance. A trivial one invites everyone to contribute, and members want to
appear engaged and useful. The result is a meeting where attention is
distributed by accessibility rather than importance.

That is the inverse of the xref:pareto-principle.adoc[*Pareto principle*],
which marks where attention would have the most leverage: a small share of
inputs produces most of the outcome. Bike shedding describes what happens
when that leverage is ignored and effort flows to whatever is easy to
discuss.

Parkinson introduced the law of triviality as a satire of bureaucratic
committees, but the same dynamic appears wherever peers review work: design
reviews, code review threads, and standards discussions all gravitate toward
naming, formatting, and other surface concerns that everyone can opine on.
Several practices blunt the effect: timeboxing the trivial items, deferring
detail to a designated owner, or adopting a
xref:daci-framework.adoc[*decision-making framework*] that assigns explicit
authority rather than opening every question to the room.

Bike shedding is often confused with
xref:parkinsons-law.adoc[*Parkinson's law*], the related but distinct adage that
"work expands to fill the time available for its completion." Both come from
the same book, but they describe different phenomena. Parkinson's law concerns
the growth of effort, while the law of triviality concerns the allocation of
attention.
