= Defense-in-depth

:link-nist-800-53:  https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/53/r5/upd1/final

*Defense-in-depth* is a xref:security.adoc[*security*] strategy that protects a system with several independent, overlapping controls, so that the failure or bypass of any one control does not lead to a total compromise. It assumes that every individual control is imperfect, and that a determined attacker will eventually find a way past at least one security control.

The idea is borrowed from military strategy, where a defender implements successive lines of defense across physical space. In software it is a core tenet of xref:secure-by-design.adoc[*secure-by-design*].

A typical web application might combine the following security controls. The controls are arranged in layers, each guarding a different bit of the path between an attacker and an asset.

* *Network.* xref:firewall.adoc[*Firewalls*], network segmentation, and xref:rate-limiting.adoc[*rate limiting*] at the edge restrict who can reach the system.

* *Transport and data.* xref:encryption.adoc[*Encryption*] in transit and at rest means that intercepted or stolen data is of little use.

* *Identity and access.* xref:authentication.adoc[*Authentication*], xref:multi-factor-authentication.adoc[*multi-factor authentication*], and xref:authorization.adoc[*authorization*] limit what any given xref:principal.adoc[*principal*] can do, even when a credential is compromised.

* *Application.* xref:input-validation.adoc[*Input validation*] and output encoding defend against injection attacks such as xref:sql-injection.adoc[*SQL injection*] and xref:cross-site-scripting.adoc[*cross-site scripting*]. xref:security-header.adoc[*Security headers*] add another layer at the client layer (in the web browser).

* *Runtime.* xref:sandbox.adoc[*Sandboxes*] or other xref:isolation.adoc[*isolation*] mechanisms contain running processes. Least-privilege permissions limit what each process can reach outside its container.

* *Detection and response.* xref:monitoring.adoc[*Monitoring*] and alerting detect intrusions. Adversarial exercises, in which a red team attacks and a blue team defends (see xref:red-team-blue-team.adoc[*red team, blue team*]), test how well this layer works in practice.

* *People and process.* Training, code review, and a xref:secure-development-lifecycle.adoc[*secure development lifecycle*] guard against the human errors that technical controls cannot catch.

The core idea is that the layers of security controls are many and diverse. There are a mix of preventive controls (to try to stop attacks), detective controls (to detect attacks, both successful or attempted), and corrective controls (to restore services and data).

Layers only add protection when they are genuinely independent. Controls in different layers should not, for example, depend on the same configuration stores, third-party libraries, or administrative accounts.

The model is often illustrated with James Reason's "Swiss cheese" model, borrowed from safety engineering. Each layer is a slice of cheese whose holes are its weaknesses. A system is vulnerable when an attacker can line-up holes though every layer of defense.

The same layering logic underlies xref:resilience.adoc[*resilience*] engineering generally, where xref:redundancy.adoc[*redundancy*] removes xref:single-point-of-failure.adoc[*single points of failure*]. The difference is that security layers must be robust against an adversary who actively probes for single points of failure.

Trade-offs include increased complexity, higher operational costs, and reduced performance. A large stack of overlapping controls can be harder to reason about than a smaller set of well-chosen ones. Furthermore, a false sense of safety can arise from defense-in-depth. It does not substitute for quickly patching known vulnerabilities, following a *secure development lifecycle*, and otherwise continuously working to reducing the attack surface.
