= Hock principle

The Hock principle is a management philosophy coined by Dee Hock, the founder and chief executive of
Visa. It states that organizations with a simple, clear purpose and principles will exhibit complex,
intelligent behavior, while organizations with complex rules and regulations will exhibit simple,
stupid behavior.

The principle inverts the usual assumption that complex problems demand complex control. Hock argued
the opposite. Specify the purpose and the principles tightly, then let the people closest to the
work decide how to act. Elaborate behavior emerges from their judgment, not from a rule book imposed
from above. Pile on rules instead, and the organization's behavior collapses into rule-following —
literal, defensive, and unable to adapt.

Hock developed the idea while building Visa in the 1970s. He designed it as a deliberately
decentralized, member-owned cooperative in which thousands of competing banks governed a single
global payment network through a thin set of shared principles rather than a central authority. The
structure held because the principles did the coordinating work that rules and oversight would
otherwise have to. In 1993, drawing on this experience, Hock introduced the term *chaordic* — a
blend of "chaos" and "order" — for organizations that are neither rigidly controlled nor anarchic,
and he expounded the philosophy in _Birth of the Chaordic Age_ (1999).

The principle is an organizational counterpart to observations about
xref:complexity.adoc[*complexity*] in systems design. Where the software complexity literature warns
that elaborate structures breed xref:big-ball-of-mud.adoc[*big balls of mud*], Hock makes the
parallel claim about organizations: heavy regulation produces stupid behavior, and the way to get
intelligent, adaptive behavior is to keep the governing constraints simple. It shares its instinct
with xref:conways-law.adoc[*Conway's law*], which treats organizational structure as the primary
design decision, and with xref:galls-law.adoc[*Gall's law*], which holds that complex working
systems grow from simple ones rather than being specified whole.
