= Artificial general intelligence (AGI)

*Artificial general intelligence (AGI)* is the hypothesised
xref:artificial-intelligence.adoc[*AI*] that can understand, learn, and perform
any intellectual task a human can, across any domain, rather than a single
narrow skill. The "general" in AGI marks the gap with today's systems: a
xref:large-language-model.adoc[*large language model*] excels at language, a
xref:computer-vision.adoc[*computer vision*] model at images, but each is
specialised to one kind of input and one family of tasks.
xref:multimodal-ai.adoc[*Multimodal AI*], which combines several modalities in a
single system, is often read as a step toward AGI – broader competence, but
still a long way short of generality.

For some researchers, AGI is the ultimate aim of all
xref:machine-learning.adoc[*machine learning*] research: a system that can do
whatever a human mind can do, and so serve as a general-purpose substrate for
intellectual work. For others, it is the doorway to the science-fiction dystopias
in which we unleash an intelligence beyond our understanding and lose control.

That fear has two familiar shapes. The first is the *intelligence explosion*:
once an AGI reaches human-level competence, it could turn its abilities to
improving itself, and each improved generation improves the next in a
xref:recursive-self-improvement.adoc[*recursive self-improvement*] loop that
runs far faster than human engineering. The second is *instrumental
convergence* – the worry that an AGI given almost any goal will pursue
sub-goals like resource acquisition and self-preservation regardless of human
welfare. The *paperclip maximiser* is the canonical thought experiment: an AI
asked only to make as many paperclips as possible diverts every resource on
Earth, humans included, to that single mission. Such scenarios are the
motivation for xref:ai-constitutionalism.adoc[*AI constitutionalism*] and the
broader field of AI safety, which asks how to specify and constrain goals
before the system is capable enough to resist correction.

AGI remains speculative. Current systems display only narrow, tool-assisted
forms of general competence – broad within a domain, but not across domains,
and never self-improving in the recursive sense. Whether AGI is a decade, a
century, or never away is itself disputed, and the disagreement is part of why
the term is so contested.
