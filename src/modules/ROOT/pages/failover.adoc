= Failover

A failover is the process of automatically or manually switching to a redundant or standby system, component, or network
upon the failure or abnormal termination of the previously active system, component, or network. It is a core mechanism
of xref:fault-tolerance.adoc[*fault tolerance*] and a standard tactic in
xref:disaster-recovery.adoc[*disaster recovery*] planning.

Failover improves the xref:availability.adoc[*availability*] of a system. It helps to remove
xref:single-point-of-failure.adoc[*single points of failure*] and keep a service operational even when some of its
components fail. To fail over at all, a system needs xref:redundancy.adoc[*redundancy*]. Standby instances are held in
reserve so that traffic can be redirected without provisioning new capacity mid-incident.

Failover is the defining mechanic of an active-passive topology, where standby instances wait idle until the active one
fails. In an xref:active-active.adoc[*active-active*] topology, by contrast, every instance serves live traffic and a
failed node's load is simply redistributed, so no failover step is required.

A failover has two phases: detecting that the active instance has failed, and promoting a standby to take its place.
Detection relies on health signals such as xref:heartbeat.adoc[*heartbeats*] and
xref:system-health-check.adoc[*health checks*]. Promotion may be automatic, driven by a
xref:load-balancer.adoc[*load balancer*] or cluster manager, or manual, requiring an operator to intervene. The faster
and more reliably both phases run, the shorter the resulting xref:downtime.adoc[*downtime*].

Failover is not instant. Depending on the detection and promotion mechanism, recovery can take anywhere from
milliseconds to minutes, during which the service is partly or wholly unavailable. A standby that has fallen behind on
xref:replication.adoc[*replication*] may also lose or reject recent writes when promoted, so the failover speed a system
can honestly claim is bounded by how current its standby state is. Because a standby usually runs the same software as
the primary, failover alone offers no protection against failures caused by a software bug. A
xref:stand-in.adoc[*stand-in*] running a different implementation can. This is why xref:fail-fast.adoc[*failing fast*]
matters. A component that fails visibly gives the failover mechanism something to detect and route around.
