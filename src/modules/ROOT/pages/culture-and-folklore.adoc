= Culture and folklore

Software development has an oral tradition alongside its formal literature: essays, origin stories, and shared
vocabulary that circulate informally among practitioners and shape how the field sees itself, without ever being
codified into a standard or a textbook. *Culture and folklore* collects the garden's entries on this informal layer. It
differs from xref:laws-and-principles.adoc[*laws and principles*], which distills a lesson into a named, quotable rule;
the entries here are closer to narrative and identity than to a rule that could be applied on a given day's work.

== Origin stories

The *garage startup* is one of the field's founding myths: a handful of people building a company from a garage or spare
room with minimal resources, a story that shaped how Silicon Valley and the wider tech industry think of themselves.
_The Cathedral and the Bazaar_ is the essay that gave the open-source movement its own founding contrast, comparing two
different ways of organizing software development, one centrally planned and one radically open.

== Vocabulary and shorthand

*Away-from-keyboard (AFK)* began in chat rooms and multiplayer games and has since become general shorthand across
text-based collaboration for a person who has stepped away. The *grug-brained developer* is a more recent piece of
vocabulary, a self-deprecating persona from a widely circulated essay that pokes fun at unnecessary complexity by
adopting the voice of an unsophisticated caveman who has, despite that, learned a few hard lessons about software.

== Canonical texts

_Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs_ (SICP) is less folklore than institution: a teaching text so
influential, and so widely referenced outside the classroom it was written for, that its nickname, "the wizard book", is
understood without explanation across much of the field.
