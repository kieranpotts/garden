= Software crisis

:link-nato-1968: http://homepages.cs.ncl.ac.uk/brian.randell/NATO/nato1968.PDF

The *software crisis* was the widely felt problem, in the 1960s and early 1970s, that software projects were routinely late, over budget, unreliable, and hard to maintain, and that hardware was advancing faster than the ability of programmers to harness it. The term gained currency at the 1968 xref:nato-software-engineering-conferences.adoc[*NATO Software Engineering Conferences*], and it is the founding problem statement of xref:software-engineering.adoc[*software engineering*] as a discipline.

The symptoms were consistent. Projects overran their schedules and budgets, and some were never delivered at all. The software that did ship was often inefficient, full of defects, and failed to meet the users' requirements. Code was difficult to change once written, so maintenance consumed a growing share of the effort.

The underlying cause was a mismatch of scale, as Edsger Dijkstra argued in his Turing Award lecture, xref:humble-programmer.adoc[*The Humble Programmer*]. As computers grew by orders of magnitude in speed and memory, the programs people wanted to write grew in step, and the ad hoc methods that worked for small programs did not scale to large systems built by large teams.

The responses to the crisis shaped the profession. The central proposal was that software production should adopt the methods of established engineering disciplines, which gave "software engineering" its name. The same period produced xref:structured-programming.adoc[*structured programming*], the phased lifecycle thinking later associated with the xref:waterfall-model.adoc[*waterfall model*], and early work in xref:formal-methods.adoc[*formal methods*] aimed at proving programs correct rather than testing them into shape.

Whether the crisis was ever resolved is contested. Frederick Brooks argued in 1986 that no single development would deliver an order-of-magnitude improvement, as set out in the essay on there being xref:silver-bullet.adoc[*no silver bullets*]. Many of the same symptoms are still reported today. Some commentators prefer to call it a chronic condition of software manufacture.
