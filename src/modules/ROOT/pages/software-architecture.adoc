= Software architecture

The *software architecture* of a system is the high-level structure of the
system: the major components it is built from, the responsibilities assigned to
each, the relationships and interactions between them, and the significant
design decisions that shape that structure. It is the part of the design that
is hardest to change once the system is built around it.

Software architecture sits at the top of the design spectrum described in
xref:architecture-and-design.adoc[*architecture and design*]. Where
xref:software-design.adoc[*software design*] – low-level
xref:design-patterns.adoc[*design patterns*] and code-level constructs – deals
with individual classes and modules, architecture deals with the system as a
whole and the boundaries between its major parts. The boundary between the two is not
sharp, and what counts as an architectural decision in one system may be a
routine design choice in another. A useful heuristic is that architecture is the
set of decisions that are expensive to reverse.

== Two views of the same thing

Software architecture is described in two complementary ways, and most modern
definitions blend the two.

* *Structure.* The system is decomposed into components – processing elements,
  data elements, and connecting elements – together with the
  xref:architectural-pattern.adoc[*architectural patterns*] and styles that
  govern how they are composed. A xref:layered-architecture.adoc[*layered architecture*], a xref:client-server-architecture.adoc[*client-server architecture*], or a xref:microservices.adoc[*microservices*] system are all
  descriptions of structure.

* *Decisions.* Architecture is also the set of significant decisions made early
  in development, the rationale for them, and the trade-offs they imply.
  Choosing a xref:monolith.adoc[*monolith*] over a distributed deployment,
  deciding how data is partitioned, or settling on a particular
  xref:event-driven-architecture.adoc[*event-driven*] style are architectural
  decisions because they constrain almost everything that follows.

The structural view answers "what does the system look like?". The decision
view answers "why does it look like that?". Both are needed: a diagram of
boxes and arrows is only half an architecture until the decisions behind it are
recorded.

== Why architecture matters

Architecture matters because it is where the system's qualities are won or
lost. Functional behavior can usually be achieved in many structures; the
qualities the system must exhibit while behaving – the
xref:quality-attributes.adoc[*quality attributes*] – are determined largely by
the architecture. These qualities, captured as
xref:non-functional-requirements.adoc[*non-functional requirements*], include
xref:software-quality.adoc[*availability*], performance, scalability, security,
modifiability, and testability. They are in frequent tension with one another,
and reconciling them is the central work of architecture. See
xref:system-design.adoc[*system design*] for a catalogue of the trade-offs that
recur.

Architecture is also the primary medium through which stakeholders
communicate about the system. Different audiences need different views:
*developers* need module structure, *operators* need deployment topology, and
*sponsors* need to see how the design meets business goals. The
xref:architectural-knowledge-management.adoc[*practice of recording architecture*] – decisions, views, and rationale, in artifacts such as
xref:c4-model.adoc[*C4 models*] and architecture decision records – is what
makes that communication possible and keeps the design legible to the people who
inherit it.

Finally, architecture is the bridge between xref:requirements.adoc[*requirements*]
and implementation. It translates what the system must do into the structures
that will do it, and it is the earliest point at which the consequences of the
requirements can be reasoned about and assessed.

== What makes a decision architectural

Not every design decision is architectural. A decision is architectural when it
satisfies at least one of the following.

* It is hard to reverse. The cost of changing it, once the system is built
  around it, would be prohibitive.
* It has system-wide effect. It shapes many components rather than one.
* It is driven by a quality attribute rather than by functional behavior.
* It resolves a significant xref:requirements.adoc[*requirement*] or constraint
  and trades one quality against another.

Decisions that are local, recoverable, or purely functional belong to design
rather than architecture. The distinction is a judgment, and it shifts with the
scale of the system.

== The elements of an architecture

Most architectural descriptions are built from a small set of recurring
elements.

* *Components* are the units that do the work – modules, services, layers,
  processes, or subsystems. Each carries a coherent responsibility.
* *Connectors* are the mechanisms by which components interact – procedure calls,
  event buses, message queues, shared databases, or network protocols.
* *Configurations* are the topological arrangements of components and
  connectors – layered, pipe-and-filter, brokered, or event-driven.
* *Properties* are the qualities the structure is intended to deliver, eg.
  low xref:coupling.adoc[*coupling*], high xref:cohesion.adoc[*cohesion*], and
  xref:conceptual-integrity.adoc[*conceptual integrity*].

These elements are realized through xref:design-principles.adoc[*design principles*] such as xref:separation-of-concerns.adoc[*separation of concerns*],
xref:abstraction.adoc[*abstraction*], and xref:encapsulation.adoc[*encapsulation*],
which together keep the structure coherent as it grows.

== What shapes an architecture

An architecture is shaped by more than its requirements. The forces that
constrain and bias it include the organization that builds it –
xref:conways-law.adoc[*Conway's law*] observes that systems mirror the
communication structures of their organizations – the existing systems and
xref:technical-debt.adoc[*technical debt*] it must accommodate, the available
technology and the skills of the team. These forces are discussed in
xref:architecture-and-design.adoc[*architecture and design*].

How much of the architecture is fixed up front, and how much is allowed to
emerge, depends on the cost of change and the stability of the requirements.
xref:big-design-up-front.adoc[*Big design up-front*] suits environments where
change is expensive and requirements are stable;
xref:evolvability.adoc[*evolvability*] suits uncertain ones. Most real systems
blend the two and develop the architecture iteratively, refining it through
xref:stepwise-refinement.adoc[*stepwise refinement*] as learning accumulates.
