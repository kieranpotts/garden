= Lady Lovelace's objection

Lady Lovelace's objection is the claim that a computer
*can only do what it is told to do, and so cannot originate anything of its own*. Alan Turing named
and discussed the objection in his 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," attributing it
to Ada Lovelace's 1843 notes on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine, in which she wrote that the
engine "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order
it to perform."

Turing treated the objection as one of the most substantial arguments against the possibility of
machine intelligence, and offered several replies. He argued that a machine can produce behavior
that surprises even its own designer, since the designer cannot always trace out every consequence
of a program's rules in advance, and that surprising the observer is close to what is meant by
originating something new. He also questioned whether the objection, if taken strictly, would deny
originality to any process, including a human brain, whose outputs are in some sense determined by
prior causes.

The objection remains a standard reference point in discussions of
xref:artificial-intelligence.adoc[*artificial intelligence*] and the *Turing test* Turing proposed
in the same paper, and it resurfaces whenever a new generation of systems, most recently
xref:large-language-model.adoc[*large language models*], prompts the question of whether output that
was not explicitly programmed counts as genuine originality or is merely a more elaborate
recombination of what the system was trained on.
