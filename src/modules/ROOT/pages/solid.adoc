= SOLID principles

SOLID is an acronym for five xref:design-principle.adoc[*design principles*] of
xref:object-oriented-programming.adoc[*object-oriented programming*], popularized by Robert C. Martin. The five
principles are the xref:single-responsibility-principle.adoc[*single responsibility principle*], the
xref:open-closed-principle.adoc[*open-closed principle*], the
xref:liskov-substitution-principle.adoc[*Liskov substitution principle*], the
xref:interface-segregation-principle.adoc[*interface segregation principle*], and
xref:dependency-inversion.adoc[*dependency inversion*].

Martin introduced the acronym in his 2000 essay _Design Principles and Design Patterns_, gathering principles he had
been writing about through the 1990s into a single mnemonic. Two of the five predate him: the Liskov substitution
principle was stated by Barbara Liskov in 1987, and the open-closed principle by Bertrand Meyer in 1988. The other three
emerged from Martin's own series of _C++ Report_ columns in 1996. The acronym itself is a teaching device, a way to make
the five memorable and to present them as a coherent set rather than as isolated heuristics.

== What the principles address

Martin framed the principles as remedies for the symptoms of poor design he called
xref:software-rot.adoc[*software rot*]: rigidity, where every change forces many others; fragility, where a change
breaks unrelated parts; immobility, where a module cannot be reused because it drags in too many dependencies; and
viscosity, where it is easier to hack around the design than to follow it. The five principles are not a checklist of
features to add but a set of preferences – for high xref:cohesion.adoc[*cohesion*], low xref:coupling.adoc[*coupling*],
and dependence on xref:abstraction.adoc[*abstractions*] rather than concretes – that, taken together, push a codebase
away from those symptoms.

The principles operate at the scale of a single class or module and its immediate collaborators. They are not
architectural rules. Styles such as xref:hexagonal-architecture.adoc[*hexagonal architecture*] and
xref:clean-architecture.adoc[*clean architecture*] apply dependency inversion across a whole system, but that is a
larger use of one SOLID letter, not a consequence of SOLID as a whole.

== How the letters relate

The five principles are interdependent, and Martin described them as building toward one another. The single
responsibility principle is the foundation: a module with one reason to change is easier to extend without modifying,
which is the goal of the open-closed principle. The open-closed principle is in turn easiest to satisfy when subtypes
are genuine behavioral substitutes (the Liskov substitution principle), when interfaces are narrow enough that every
implementer can honor the full contract (the interface segregation principle), and when callers depend on abstractions
owned by the caller rather than on concrete implementations (dependency inversion). Each letter makes the next more
achievable.

xref:dependency-injection.adoc[*Dependency injection*] is the technique most commonly used to put dependency inversion
into practice, and so it is the mechanism by which much of SOLID becomes operational in real codebases.

== Relation to other heuristics

SOLID overlaps with xref:grasp.adoc[*GRASP*], Craig Larman's set of responsibility-assignment patterns, which express
similar preferences for low coupling and high cohesion but frame them as questions about which class should own a
responsibility. SOLID is also a specific application of xref:separation-of-concerns.adoc[*separation of concerns*] to
the class and module level. The xref:law-of-demeter.adoc[*Law of Demeter*] is a complementary heuristic: it constrains
how an object talks to its collaborators, where the SOLID letters constrain the shape of the modules that collaborate.

== Criticism

The principles are heuristics, not laws, and they are easy to over-apply. Introducing an abstraction for every
collaboration, ahead of any second implementation, produces empty interfaces and speculative hierarchies that multiply
without earning their keep. The result is xref:complexity.adoc[*complexity*] in the name of avoiding it.
xref:yagni.adoc[*YAGNI*] is the usual counterweight: an abstraction that never sees a second implementation is
generality bought on credit. The principles are most useful when applied where a dependency is genuinely volatile or
where xref:unit-testing.adoc[*unit testing*] needs a seam, not as a blanket rule over every class.
