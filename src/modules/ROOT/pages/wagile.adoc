= Wagile

:link-forrester: https://www.forrester.com/report/water-scrum-fall-is-the-reality-of-agile-for-most-organizations-today/RES60109
:link-infoq:     https://www.infoq.com/news/2011/12/water-scrum-fall-is-the-norm
:link-wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagile

Wagile, a portmanteau of "waterfall" and "agile," is a label for software development approaches that combine the xref:waterfall-model.adoc[*waterfall model*] with xref:agile.adoc[*agile*] practices. The term is usually pejorative. It describes an organization that has adopted agile's visible practices – eg. sprints, stand-ups, backlogs – while the planning, funding, and release processes around them remain plan-driven and sequential.

The best-known description of the pattern is *Water-Scrum-Fall*, a term coined by Forrester analyst Dave West in a 2011 report, _Water-Scrum-Fall Is the Reality of Agile for Most Organizations Today_. West observed that most enterprises claiming to be agile had changed only the middle part of their delivery process. He observed three distinct phases.

1. *Water.* An up-front planning phase, negotiated between the business and IT, fixes requirements, budget, and delivery date before development starts. This is xref:big-design-up-front.adoc[*big design up-front*] applied to scope and funding.

2. *Scrum.* The development team works iteratively, often using xref:scrum.adoc[*Scrum*], to deliver the scope that the plan has already committed to.

3. *Fall.* The finished work passes through a gated, infrequent release process – change-approval boards, a xref:code-freeze.adoc[*code freeze*], a xref:big-bang.adoc[*big bang*] deployment – run by a separate operations function.

West did not condemn the model outright. His argument was that the boundaries between the stages matter. Teams that let the "Water" and the "Fall" grow unchecked keep the costs of agile ceremonies, but forfeit most of agile's benefits.

The core criticism is that wagile shortens the feedback loop where it matters least. Iterations inside the development phase produce working software as short intervals, but that software reaches real users much later. Because scope was fixed at the start, what the team learns during iterations cannot change the scope — this feedback loop is absent. In addition, risk is deferred to the end of the project.

Wagile overlaps with xref:flaccid-agile.adoc[*flaccid agile*], but the two diagnose different gaps. Flaccid agile is a team that runs agile ceremonies, without the engineering practices of *extreme programming* that underpin agile *ways of working*.

Wagile is rarely designed as a deliberate process. Rather, it is what remains when agile is adopted bottom-up by development teams, while the surrounding organization keeps the structures it already had. Annual budgeting and fixed-price contracts demand a committed scope and date up-front. Regulators and auditors may expect phase-gate documentation. Operations teams that sit apart from development keep their own release cadence and approval controls. Each of these pulls the start and the end of the process back toward waterfall, whatever happens in between.

Alternatively, the wagile pattern can be a symptom of xref:cargo-culting.adoc[*cargo-culting*], in which an organization copies agile's rituals but not its values.

Remedies require redesign to the first and third phases of the process. xref:devops.adoc[*DevOps*] and xref:continuous-delivery.adoc[*continuous delivery*] put the path to production in the team's own hands, so that releases become frequent and routine rather than gated events. And funding persistent product teams rather than fixed-scope projects, and planning against outcomes rather than feature lists, lets what is learned in iterations feed back into scope.
