= The humble programmer

:link-ewd340: https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD03xx/EWD340.html

*The Humble Programmer* is the title of the lecture Edsger W. Dijkstra gave on 14 August 1972, in Boston, on receiving the ACM A.M. Turing Award. Published in _Communications of the ACM_ in October 1972, it is one of the most quoted essays in computing. Its thesis is that programming is an intellectual activity whose central difficulty is the smallness of the human mind, so the best programmers are those who accept that limit and organize their work around it.

Dijkstra opened with a diagnosis of the xref:software-crisis.adoc[*software crisis*]. The major cause, he said, was that the machines had become several orders of magnitude more powerful. As long as there were no machines, he said, then programming was no problem at all, and when there were a few weak computers it was a mild problem. Now that there were gigantic computers, programming had become an equally gigantic problem. The difficulty lay in the scale of xref:complexity.adoc[*complexity*] that programmers were being asked to master, not in the machines.

His answer was humility. A competent programmer, he argued, is fully aware of the strictly limited size of their own brain, and so approaches the task with modest means and avoids clever tricks. That humility has practical consequences.

* *Correctness by construction.* Programs should be composed so as to be xref:correctness.adoc[*correct*] from the start, with the proof and the program developed hand-in-hand, rather than written carelessly and debugged into shape. He observed that testing can show the presence of bugs but never their absence, which is the argument for the discipline later pursued in xref:formal-methods.adoc[*formal methods*].

* *Structured programming.* The program text should be organized so that the programmer can reason about it, and the lecture is an early statement of the case for xref:structured-programming.adoc[*structured programming*], including the avoidance of the `goto` statement.

* *Abstraction and layering.* A system too large to hold in one head must be divided into levels, each understood in isolation. This is the idea behind xref:abstraction.adoc[*abstraction*] and xref:stepwise-refinement.adoc[*stepwise refinement*].

* *Simple, disciplined languages and tools.* Dijkstra criticized languages that were large and baroque, and held that a tool should help the programmer think rather than tempt them into complexity.

The lecture is also remembered for its prose, and its tone is as much a part of the argument as its technical content. Dijkstra was skeptical of the engineering optimism of the time, and he treated the crisis as a problem of discipline rather than of tooling. Frederick Brooks's later argument in the xref:silver-bullet.adoc[*silver bullet*] essay reaches a similar conclusion from a different direction.
