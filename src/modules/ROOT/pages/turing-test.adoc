= Turing test

:link-turing-paper:      https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/LIX.236.433
:link-jones-bergen-2025: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674

The Turing test is a proposed measure of machine intelligence in which a human judge converses in text with an unseen partner and tries to tell whether it is a person or a machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell, the machine is said to have passed. Alan Turing proposed it in 1950 in _Computing Machinery and Intelligence_, where he called it the *imitation game*.

Turing introduced the game to sidestep a question he thought too vague to answer: "Can machines think?" In its place he asked whether a machine could behave, in conversation, indistinguishably from a person. The test therefore measures _behavior_, not what lies behind it. Passing shows that a simulation is convincing. It says nothing about whether there is comprehension, intent, or experience behind the words.

Turing anticipated several objections to the possibility of machine intelligence and answered them in the same paper, including the one now known as xref:lady-lovelaces-objection.adoc[*Lady Lovelace's objection*].

For most of the test's history, nothing came close. Early chatbots such as ELIZA could hold a judge's attention only briefly, and then mostly because people are inclined to read minds into anything that talks — see xref:eliza-effect.adoc[*the ELIZA effect*]. That changed with xref:large-language-model.adoc[*large language models*]. In a 2025 study by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen, judges in a three-party version of the test, talking to a person and a model at the same time, picked a model prompted to adopt a persona as the human more often than they picked the real human.
