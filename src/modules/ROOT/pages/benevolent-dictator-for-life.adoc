= Benevolent dictator for life (BDFL)

:link-pep-13:    https://peps.python.org/pep-0013/

A *benevolent dictator for life (BDFL)* is the founder or lead of an *open-source* project who holds final authority over its direction and resolves disputes by decree, when consensus cannot be reached.

The title is tongue-in-cheek and describes a xref:governance.adoc[*governance*] model in which decision rights are concentrated in a single person rather than shared among a committee or voted on by members. The "benevolent" part is a claim about trust. The community tolerates the dictator because they are seen to act in the project's interest rather than their own.

The model is distinct from governance by consensus, where decisions are put to a vote or require broad agreement.

The term is generally traced to the Python community in the mid-1990s, where it was applied to Guido van Rossum, the creator of the language. The model was already common in open-source projects by then, and has been associated with other long-running projects since, including the Linux kernel under Linus Torvalds, Perl under Larry Wall, and Ruby under Yukihiro "Matz" Matsumoto.

In practice most BDFLs do not decide everything. They delegate day-to-day decisions to trusted maintainers, and reserve their authority for contested or architecturally significant questions. The xref:linux.adoc[*Linux*] kernel's "lieutenants" system is the classic example, with changes flowing up through a chain of subsystem maintainers who each answer to the person above them.

The main benefit of the model is xref:conceptual-integrity.adoc[*conceptual integrity*]. A single authority with a coherent vision can say no to features that would pull a design in conflicting directions, and can make a call quickly instead of waiting for a committee to settle. The same argument underpins the xref:surgical-team.adoc[*surgical team*], and Brooks's case in _The Mythical Man-Month_ that a design is best held in one mind.

Decisions are also cheaper. There is no voting procedure to run, and no coalition to build.

The model is also self-limiting in open source, because the code is open. If the community loses confidence in the dictator, the ultimate check is a **fork**. A dissatisfied faction copies the project and takes it in its own direction. The threat of a fork keeps a BDFL accountable, even though nobody can vote them out.

But the model has a number of weaknesses. A project's direction, and often its deepest knowledge, rests with one person — a form of the xref:bus-factor.adoc[*bus factor*] problem. All contested decisions queue up behind one person, who is often an unpaid volunteer. Fatigue and abuse from the community are common reasons BDFLs step down.

"For life" is rarely literal, and a project that never planned for the dictator's departure may be left without a legitimate way to make decisions. Python faced this in July 2018, when van Rossum stepped down after a bruising dispute over assignment expressions (PEP 572). The project replaced him with a five-person elected steering council, defined in {link-pep-13}[PEP 13].

Some projects soften the model. A BDFL may delegate to a standing group of core developers, as in the "BDFL-delegate" role that Python used to hand individual proposals to a trusted reviewer. Others use a BDFL for the early years and later move to an elected council, a foundation, or a rotating maintainer role.
